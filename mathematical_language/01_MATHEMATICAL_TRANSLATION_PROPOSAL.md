## 0. Summary

1. **Most of the music idea can be translated into exact mathematics.** Rows become sets of time points. A "uniform column structure" becomes an arithmetic lattice $\varphi + w\mathbb{Z}$. "Fitting" becomes containment in a lattice. Once "fit" is defined, the merger of Rows 2 and 3 is no longer a free design choice. **The merger follows from the definition** (Proposition 2).
2. **The linear and curved idea can be stated exactly** using second differences. One proposition gives an exact form of the Author's claim that the curve "stays low longer" (Proposition 4). Another explains why a terminal gesture spanning a single column can never look curved (Proposition 5). This connects Statement 5 with Statement 30.
3. **Statement 32 has a precise and faithful meaning.** "Row 1's columns can't be divided by anything but itself" means that the terminal gesture's column count $N_1$ admits no nontrivial equal regrouping. That holds exactly when $N_1$ is prime (Proposition 6). There is a caveat: this only makes sense relative to the coarsest fit.

## 1. The objects

### Definition 1 (Piece, time, events)
A **piece** occupies the time interval $[0, T]$ with $T > 0$.
A **sound event** is a pair $e = (s_e, f_e)$ with $0 \le s_e < f_e \le T$ (start, finish). Any further attributes, such as pitch or loudness, are recorded but not used unless stated.

### Definition 2 (Group, row, boundary set)
A **group** $G$ is a finite non-empty set of events. Its **span** is $[\min_{e\in G} s_e,\ \max_{e \in G} f_e]$.
A **row** $R_r$ ($r = 1, 2, 3, \dots$) is a finite sequence of groups.
The **boundary set** of a row is the set $B_r \subset [0,T]$ of all start and end points of its groups' spans.

> **Question Q1.** Should $B_r$ contain only *group* boundaries, or also the start and end of every *individual sound* in the row? Statement 28 says the columns "divide into each row music group evenly", which suggests group boundaries. I use group boundaries below, but every result also holds with the other choice.

### Definition 3 (Uniform column structure)
For an offset $\varphi \in \mathbb{R}$ and a width $w > 0$, the **uniform column structure** $\Lambda(\varphi, w)$ consists of the columns
$$C_k = [\varphi + k w,\ \varphi + (k+1) w), \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z},$$
whose borders form the **lattice** $\varphi + w\mathbb{Z} = \{\varphi + k w : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.
Every column has the same width, so the column-width variance is $\sigma^2 = 0$ by construction.

### Definition 4 (Exact fit) — *the central definition*
A row $R_r$ **fits** $\Lambda(\varphi, w)$ when every boundary lies on a column border:
$$B_r \subseteq \varphi + w\mathbb{Z}.$$
In words, every group in the row starts and ends exactly on a column line. Each group then spans a whole number of columns, so the columns "divide into each row music group evenly" (Statement 28).

### Definition 5 (Difference group)
$D_r$ is the set of all sums and differences, with integer coefficients, of the numbers $b - b'$ for $b, b' \in B_r$. In symbols, $D_r = \langle\, b - b' : b, b' \in B_r \,\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}$, the additive subgroup of $\mathbb{R}$ generated by the gaps between boundaries.

### Fact F1 (subgroups of the real line)
- Every subgroup of $\mathbb{R}$ generated by finitely many **rational** numbers is **cyclic**: it equals $g\mathbb{Z}$ for one $g > 0$. That $g$ is the rational gcd of the generators.
- A subgroup generated by two numbers whose ratio is **irrational** is **dense** in $\mathbb{R}$, so it is not of the form $g\mathbb{Z}$.
- Every subgroup of a cyclic group $w\mathbb{Z}$ is itself cyclic.

## 2. Part I — The mathematics of the music idea

### 2.1 Stage A: each row's own uniform columns (Statements 16, 28, 29)

**Proposition 1 (Which widths fit a row).** Let $B_r$ have at least two points. Then
$$\exists\, \varphi:\ B_r \subseteq \varphi + w\mathbb{Z} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D_r \subseteq w\mathbb{Z}.$$

*Proof.* (⇒) If every $b \in B_r$ equals $\varphi + k_b w$, then $b - b' = (k_b - k_{b'})w \in w\mathbb{Z}$. Since $w\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under integer combinations, $D_r \subseteq w\mathbb{Z}$.
(⇐) Choose any $b_0 \in B_r$ and set $\varphi = b_0$. For every $b$, we have $b - b_0 \in D_r \subseteq w\mathbb{Z}$, so $b \in \varphi + w\mathbb{Z}$. ∎

**Corollary 1.1 (The natural width of a row).** Suppose all boundaries are rational numbers. This is always true for MIDI ticks and written sheet music. Then $D_r = g_r\mathbb{Z}$, and the widths that fit Row $r$ are **exactly**
$$w \in \left\{ g_r,\ \tfrac{g_r}{2},\ \tfrac{g_r}{3},\ \dots \right\}.$$
So every row has one **coarsest** fitting width $g_r$, and every other fitting width is an equal subdivision of it.

*I guess this $g_r$ is what the Author means by "Row 2 has its own unique set of uniform columns that fit the entire set of sounds in the row" (Statement 29). It is unique, uniform, determined by the whole row, and not chosen arbitrarily.*

**Corollary 1.2 (When no exact fit exists).** Suppose two boundary gaps have an irrational ratio. This can happen with times measured from a real recording. Then $D_r$ is dense by Fact F1, so no width $w > 0$ fits exactly.
→ Exact fit is a **notated-music** concept. Recorded music would need an approximate fit (§2.6, open question O1).

**Example (README §3, the 4 and 3 example).** On a 12-unit passage:
- Row 2 has boundaries $\{0,4,8,12\}$, so $D_2 = 4\mathbb{Z}$ and $g_2 = 4$.
- Row 3 has boundaries $\{0,3,6,9,12\}$, so $D_3 = 3\mathbb{Z}$ and $g_3 = 3$.

### 2.2 Stage B: the merger (Statements 18, 28, 29, 30)

**Definition 6 (Common fit).** A uniform structure $\Lambda(\varphi, w)$ **fits Rows 2 and 3 together** if $B_2 \cup B_3 \subseteq \varphi + w\mathbb{Z}$.

**Proposition 2 (The merger is forced by the definition of fit).** Let
$$D_{23} = D_2 + D_3 + (b_2 - b_3)\mathbb{Z}$$
for any $b_2 \in B_2$ and $b_3 \in B_3$. The result does not depend on which $b_2$ and $b_3$ are chosen. Then:
1. A common fit exists if and only if $D_{23}$ is cyclic, $D_{23} = w_1 \mathbb{Z}$. This is always true for rational boundaries.
2. $w_1$ is the coarsest common width, and every common width is $w_1 / n$.
3. If the two rows share at least one boundary, for example both start at $0$, then $w_1 = \gcd(g_2, g_3)$.
4. $w_1$ divides both $g_2$ and $g_3$. So each of Row 2's own columns is exactly $g_2 / w_1$ merged columns, and each of Row 3's own columns is exactly $g_3/w_1$ merged columns. **Both native structures survive intact inside the merged one.**

*Proof.* Apply Proposition 1 to the single set $B_2 \cup B_3$. Its difference group is generated by the within-row gaps, which give $D_2$ and $D_3$, together with the cross-row gaps $b - b'$. Every cross-row gap equals $(b_2 - b_3)$ plus an element of $D_2 + D_3$. That gives (1) and (2), using Fact F1. For (3), a shared boundary means $b_2 = b_3$ can be chosen, so $D_{23} = g_2\mathbb{Z} + g_3\mathbb{Z} = \gcd(g_2,g_3)\mathbb{Z}$. For (4), $D_2 \subseteq D_{23} = w_1\mathbb{Z}$, so $g_2 \in w_1\mathbb{Z}$, and the same holds for $g_3$. ∎

**Example.** $g_2 = 4$ and $g_3 = 3$ give $w_1 = \gcd(4,3) = 1$. Row 2's columns are 4 merged columns wide, Row 3's are 3, and Row 1 receives width 1.

*I guess this matches Statement 18 closely:* "both rows share the same fundamental column size making the two columns width … one row … width 4 and another … width 3 … so the two columns share a same column design." On this reading, 4 and 3 are both built from the shared size 1.

**Relation to the historical result.** The old boundary union $\{0,3,4,6,8,9,12\}$, with unequal slices $[3,1,2,2,1,3]$, lies entirely on the merged lattice $\mathbb{Z}$. **Proposition 2 says the merged structure is the coarsest uniform structure that contains the old union.** The README's concern that the old union was non-uniform is resolved by this, not contradicted.

**The alternative reading (needs a decision).** "Fit" could instead mean a **common enclosing cycle**: one big column that contains whole groups of *both* rows. That gives width $\operatorname{lcm}(g_2,g_3)$, which is 12 in the example. Both readings are mathematically valid. They answer different questions:

| Reading | Width | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Common subdivision (refinement) | $\gcd(g_2, g_3)$ | The smallest shared building block |
| Common cycle (coarsening) | $\operatorname{lcm}(g_2, g_3)$ | When both rows come back into line together |

> **Question Q2.** Which one is Row 1's column? *I guess the common subdivision (gcd), because of Statements 17 and 18.* The README deliberately left this open, so the decision is the Author's.

### 2.3 Uniformity check (JOB-11)
Under Definitions 3–6, uniformity ($\sigma^2 = 0$) is **automatic**: every column of a lattice has the same width. The real content of JOB-11 is therefore the *fit*, not the uniformity. Under exact fit, the fit is a yes/no property. Under approximate fit (§2.6), it becomes a residual that has to be measured.

### 2.4 Stage C: Row 1 (Statements 2, 3, 11, 14, 30)

**Definition 7 (Row 1's structure).** Row 1 receives $\Lambda(\varphi_1, w_1)$ from Proposition 2. It is not chosen in advance. This preserves the README's order of operations: Row 1 receives the structure, it does not impose it.

**Consequence.** Different pieces have different $g_2$ and $g_3$, so they get different $w_1$. This matches "Row 1 column size differs between music" (Statement 30) and "the columns fit rows 1, 2, 3, but not across music songs" (Statement 11).

The *constant style or feeling* that binds the rows (Statements 3 and 14) has **no mathematical translation yet**. *I guess it could become an invariant: a quantity that stays the same across every group of the piece.* No such quantity is proposed here.

### 2.5 Stage D: the terminal low-to-high graph (Statements 4, 5, 12, 23, 26, 30)

**Definition 8 (Terminal interval and column count).** The terminal gesture occupies $[t_a, t_b]$, with both ends on Row 1's lattice (Statement 23 puts it at the tail end). Its **column count** is
$$N_1 = \frac{t_b - t_a}{w_1} \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}.$$

**Definition 9 (Height and trajectory).** A **height** function $h$ assigns to each time a "how high" value. The **trajectory** is the list of points $(k, y_k)$ with $y_k = h(t_a + k w_1)$ for $k = 0, \dots, N_1$. The Author says "from low row to high row" (Statement 7). There are two candidates for $h$:
- (a) the row index: *I guess* the sound moves upward through the rows;
- (b) a pitch-register value (the README says this is **not** agreed).

> **Question Q3.** What is "high"? Is it the row number, the pitch, or something else?

**Definition 10 (Linear and curved).** Write $\Delta^2 y_k = y_{k+1} - 2y_k + y_{k-1}$ for the second difference.
- The trajectory is **linear** if $\Delta^2 y_k = 0$ for every $k$. Equal steps: it rises by the same amount each column.
- It is **curved** otherwise.
- It is **convex** ("stays low, then rises") if $\Delta^2 y_k \ge 0$ for all $k$ and is curved.

**Proposition 3 (The column size does not create the shape).** If $h$ is a straight line over $[t_a,t_b]$, the trajectory is linear for **every** $w_1$. If $h$ is strictly convex and $N_1 \ge 2$, the trajectory is curved for every $w_1$.
*Proof.* A straight line sampled at equal steps rises by equal amounts, so $\Delta^2 y = 0$. For strictly convex $h$, the middle sample lies strictly below the chord through its neighbours, so $\Delta^2 y_k > 0$. ∎
→ This confirms the README's warning that a uniform grid alone cannot decide linear or curved. The shape comes from $h$, the music, and not from $w_1$.

**Proposition 4 (A curve stays low longer).** This is an exact form of Statements 5 and 12. Normalize the gesture so it runs from height 0 to height 1 over the interval $[0,1]$. Let $f$ be the normalized height function. If $f$ is convex, then $f(x) \le x$ for all $x$. Consequently, the number of columns spent in the lower half,
$$L(f) = \#\{k : f(k/N_1) < \tfrac12\},$$
is at least the number a straight line spends there.
*Proof.* Convexity gives $f(x) \le (1-x)f(0) + x f(1) = x$. Every column where the line is below ½ is therefore also a column where $f$ is below ½. ∎

*I guess this is what Statement 5 means by "a mixture of musical styles has to go low, stay low on the timeline to go high", and what Statement 12 means by "the curved has more on the lower row hence it has more columns connecting to the row".*

**Proposition 5 (One column can never look curved).** If $N_1 = 1$, the trajectory has two points and is automatically linear. Curvature can only be seen when $N_1 \ge 2$.
*Proof.* Definition 10 needs three points to form a second difference. ∎

*I guess this connects Statement 5, "traditional music can go low row to single column but a mixture … has to go several columns", with Statement 30.* A gesture that completes in one column is linear by necessity. A gesture spread over several columns *can* show its curve. This is a derived relationship, not a musical finding.

### 2.6 Open mathematical problem O1: approximate fit
For recorded music (Corollary 1.2), define the fit error
$$\varepsilon_r(\varphi, w) = \max_{b \in B_r} \operatorname{dist}(b,\ \varphi + w\mathbb{Z}).$$
**Difficulty:** for any tolerance $\tau > 0$, every width $w \le 2\tau$ fits within $\tau$. So "the widths that fit" always includes all very small widths, and something must reward coarseness. One option is the coarsest $w$ with $\varepsilon_r \le \tau$. Whether this is well-defined and stable is an **open question**. It should be settled before recorded music is discussed.

### 2.7 Journey and outcome (Statements 22, 24, 25, 26, 28)

**Definition 11 (Journey arithmetic).** Rows 2 and 3 each contribute one completed segment of the journey, each counted as $1$. The target in Row 1 is their sum, $1 + 1 = 2$ (Statement 24). If the segments differ, write $a + b$.

**Definition 12 (Outcome).** $\beta \in \{0, 1\}$, where $\beta = 1$ means the goal was reached well ("the harvest, the win") and $\beta = 0$ means a sad ending ("aww"), as in Statement 25.

**Hypothesis (Author, Statement 26):** $\beta = 1 \iff$ the terminal trajectory is linear. Since Definition 10 applies only to $[t_a, t_b]$, this is consistent with "linear at the start and during training", where the shape only changes at the end.

> **Question Q4.** Are Rows 2 and 3 **simultaneous** layers (both sounding at once) or **consecutive** stages (A, then B)? Statement 23 says "group a sound playing into group b sound", which suggests consecutive. The merger idea suggests simultaneous. Propositions 1–2 work either way, but the meaning of $1 + 1$ depends on the answer.

## 3. Part II — Number theory and the Riemann hypothesis (established facts)

**F2 (Prime).** An integer $p \ge 2$ is prime when its only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$: $D(p) = \{1, p\}$.

**F3 (Zeta function).** For $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$:
$$\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^{-s} = \prod_{p\ \text{prime}} \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}}.$$
The product form, due to Euler, is the reason zeta is tied to the primes. The function extends to all complex $s \ne 1$.

**F4 (Zeros).** The **trivial zeros** are $s = -2, -4, -6, \dots$. All other zeros, the **non-trivial** ones, lie in the strip $0 < \operatorname{Re}(s) < 1$.

**F5 (Riemann hypothesis — unproven).** Every non-trivial zero has $\operatorname{Re}(s) = \tfrac12$. An equivalent statement (von Koch, 1901) is
$$\pi(x) = \operatorname{Li}(x) + O(\sqrt{x}\,\log x),$$
where $\pi(x)$ counts the primes up to $x$. In plain words, **the RH says the primes are spread out as evenly as they possibly could be.**

**F6 (Coprimality and $\zeta(2)$).** The probability that two "randomly chosen" positive integers have gcd $1$ is $1/\zeta(2) = 6/\pi^2 \approx 0.608$. More generally, the probability that their gcd equals $d$ is $1/(d^2\,\zeta(2))$. This is a natural-density statement.

**F7 (Primes thin out).** Near $N$, roughly one integer in $\ln N$ is prime (the prime number theorem).

## 4. Part II — Connecting the music to the number theory

### 4.1 What "Row 1 is like a prime" means (Statement 32)

**Proposition 6 (Indivisible terminal gesture).** The terminal gesture's $N_1$ columns can be regrouped into equal blocks of $d$ columns exactly when $d$ divides $N_1$. Therefore:
$$\text{the only equal regroupings are "all } N_1 \text{ together" and "one column each"} \iff N_1 \text{ is prime (or } N_1 = 1).$$
*Proof.* Equal blocks of $d$ columns tile $N_1$ columns exactly when $N_1 = d \cdot m$ for an integer $m$. ∎

*I guess this is the faithful mathematical form of "the low to high in row 1 is the columns that can't be divided by anything but itself".*

**Caveat (important).** $N_1$ depends on which lattice Row 1 uses. Subdividing Row 1 by a factor $n$ multiplies $N_1$ by $n$, and then $N_1$ is composite whenever $n \ge 2$ and $N_1 \ge 2$. **Primality only has meaning relative to the coarsest fit $w_1$ of Proposition 2.** This is one reason Corollary 1.1 matters.

### 4.2 The "1" and the "2" (Statements 24 and 32 together)
- Statement 32: the finish line is $1$.
- Statement 24: the target is $1 + 1 = 2$.

These do not conflict. $1$ counts the *endpoints*, one arrival. $2$ counts the *segments*, two journeys.

*I guess (analogy, not a theorem):* $2$ is itself the smallest prime, with $D(2) = \{1, 2\}$. Its two divisors are exactly "the one endpoint" (1) and "the whole journey" (2). In this sense the target $1 + 1 = 2$ already carries the structure the Author described in Statement 32. It is still only an analogy.

### 4.3 How Rows 2 and 3 might fit in (the Author's open question)
Under Proposition 2, the interaction of Rows 2 and 3 is $w_1 = \gcd(g_2, g_3)$. Measured in a reference unit $u$, write $m = g_2/u$ and $n = g_3/u$. Fact F6 then says that among all pairs of integer row widths, a fraction $6/\pi^2$ are coprime and merge into width $u$ itself. **This is a genuine, established link between "two rows merging" and the zeta function.**

**But it is the wrong part of zeta for the RH.** F6 uses the *value* $\zeta(2)$. The Riemann hypothesis concerns the *zeros* (F5). I know of no mathematics that leads from gcds of row widths to the location of the zeros. It would be dishonest to suggest otherwise.

**A note on counting.** By F7, a typical $N_1$ near 10 is prime about 40% of the time ($1/\ln 10 \approx 0.43$). Finding prime column counts in some examples would therefore mean nothing without comparing against a baseline. The README already says this in its Section 11.

> **Open question O2 (mathematical).** Is there *any* natural family of row structures whose counts behave like $\pi(x)$? Only that would let the RH's prime-spacing statement (F5) say something about music. This is the honest form of the Author's question "how would rows 2 and 3 fit the Riemann hypothesis?"

## 5. Part III and Part IV — Preview only (not started)

**Part III (storytelling, Statements 20–26, 31).** A journey is a path $A = x_0 \to x_1 \to \dots \to x_m = B$ in a set of story states. The goal is a yes/no property of $B$, and the outcome is $\beta \in \{0,1\}$ (Definition 12). A "catalogue" (Statement 31) is a finite set of pairs (story, music) together with a relation saying which pairs belong together. A *new* pair is "logical like previous books" if it satisfies the same relation. The AI-training part of Statement 31 is outside the rules of this study and is not translated.

**Part IV (a common language).** *I guess* all three subjects share one skeleton: **segments → combine → one terminal event → outcome.** In music this is Rows 2 and 3 → merge → Row 1 gesture → linear or curved. In arithmetic it is $1 + 1 \to 2$, with divisors $\{1, 2\}$. In a story it is A → B → arrival → $\beta$. Whether this skeleton is a real shared structure or only an analogy is exactly what Part IV should investigate. It is not claimed here.
